## Abstract

Interpersonal dynamics are heavily influenced by established behavioral baselines, often leading to asymmetric social judgments. This theoretical paper explores the psychological phenomenon wherein consistently accommodating individuals (the "prosocial actors") are harshly penalized for isolated boundary-setting, while habitually unaccommodating individuals (the "self-serving actors") receive disproportionate praise for minimal prosocial behavior. Drawing upon Expectancy Violations Theory (Burgoon) and the Contrast Effect, we examine how societal expectations trap highly empathetic individuals in cycles of "pathological altruism" (Oakley).

Furthermore, this paper introduces the "Cliff vs. Ground" cognitive distortion model to analyze the internal dilemma of the overthinker. Driven by conflict aversion and sociotropy, the overthinker catastrophizes (Beck) interpersonal requests, treating every boundary as a life-or-death "cliff" scenario where saying no equates to letting the other person fall. This anxiety forces compliance, resulting in a lose-lose dynamic of exhaustion and resentment. We distinguish this paranoia from the "ground" reality, wherein reasonable actors gracefully accept boundaries without relational damage. 

Ultimately, this paper argues that resolving this dilemma requires a paradigm shift: abandoning the conflict-avoidant, people-pleasing persona of the "nice guy" in favor of the boundary-driven morality of the "good person." By breaking the illusion of nobility in endless accommodation, individuals can dismantle expectancy traps and establish sustainable, authentic relationships.
